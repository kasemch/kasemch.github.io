Designing Educational and Research Management Systems with Neon PostgreSQL: A Data Governance and Evidence-Driven Development Perspective

Kasem Chooratna · Faculty of Education, Ramkhamhaeng University, Thailand

Version 1.1 · September 2026 · Non-production
Scope and status: Conceptual and practice-oriented discussion of ongoing prototype development. Not a peer-reviewed research article, production certification, institutional endorsement, or report of independently verified security outcomes.

Abstract

Educational and research management systems require structured data management, access control, evidence traceability, and responsible software development. This practice-oriented technical case study examines a conceptual architecture using Neon PostgreSQL for non-production educational and research management prototypes. It discusses relational data design, application authorization, database access policies, audit events, and synthetic-data testing across two illustrative domains. Architectural intentions are distinguished from implemented and verified functionality. No experimental evaluation, production deployment, or measured performance improvement is claimed.

1. Introduction

Universities manage interconnected information across teaching, research, and quality assurance. Project statuses and academic decisions should be traceable to identifiable records rather than isolated dashboard labels. PostgreSQL supports relational constraints, transactions, roles, and row-level security (PostgreSQL Global Development Group, n.d.). Neon provides managed PostgreSQL infrastructure and database branching (Neon, n.d.). These capabilities are relevant to prototype design, but platform selection alone does not establish security or institutional readiness.

2. Objectives

This article aims to (a) examine the role of Neon PostgreSQL in educational and research prototype architecture; (b) describe the integration of relational structures, authorization, evidence traceability, and auditability; (c) discuss isolated development and synthetic-data testing; and (d) identify verification requirements before deployment. It does not establish comparative effectiveness or educational impact.

3. Development Context

The case concerns ongoing non-production prototype work associated with academic activities at Ramkhamhaeng University, Thailand. The research-management domain considers projects, milestones, document registers, evidence references, and approval states. The educational-activity domain considers participation states, assessment records, and access boundaries. These are illustrative design domains, not evidence of institution-wide implementation or authorized processing of real learner records.

4. Conceptual Architecture

The proposed architecture comprises five layers: presentation interfaces; an application layer for identity checks, validation, authorization, and workflow transitions; a relational data layer for structured records and constraints; a governance layer for ownership, provenance, roles, and access policies; and a verification layer for synthetic fixtures, migration review, negative tests, and audit inspection. The browser must not be the sole enforcement point for protected information. Database roles and row-level security complement rather than replace application-level controls.

5. Evidence-Driven Development

Design specification, implemented functionality, and verified functionality are distinct states. A policy document is not proof that unauthorized access is prevented; that claim requires relevant execution records under the intended roles and configuration. Likewise, a successful migration is not evidence of complete application security. Each technical claim should be associated with an implementation artifact, test procedure, observed result, and release decision. No unverified test outcome is presented here as a result.

6. Branching and Synthetic Data

Isolated database environments can support migration inspection and controlled experiments before production decisions. Synthetic records enable selected workflows to be tested without publishing student or participant information. They do not necessarily reproduce real-world data complexity, so passing synthetic tests cannot establish operational readiness.

7. Security and Data Governance

Authentication and authorization serve different purposes. The design calls for explicit tests of unauthenticated requests, cross-user record access, attempts to exceed assigned privileges, and unauthorized workflow transitions. Public client resources must not contain database credentials. Audit events should support accountability without exposing secrets. These are requirements and proposed test scenarios, not claims of independently certified security.

8. Academic and Practical Implications

A relational database can support the organization of academic evidence and operational records, but trustworthy decisions also depend on data quality, legitimate authority, and reliable access controls. Integration across teaching, research, and quality assurance must preserve domain-specific responsibilities. Educational effectiveness and institutional impact would require separate empirical evaluation.

9. Limitations

This is a conceptual and practice-oriented case, not a controlled empirical study. It reports no comparative benchmarks, quantitative performance measurements, user-experience findings, or independent security certification. Synthetic-data testing has limited generalizability. The discussion does not establish institutional endorsement or production deployment.

10. Conclusion

Neon PostgreSQL is a potential relational foundation for academic information-system prototypes. Responsible development also requires coherent schemas, authorization boundaries, evidence lineage, reproducible verification, and explicit release governance. Future work should document implementation outcomes and evaluate operational suitability before use with real institutional records.
